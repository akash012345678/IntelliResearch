Abstract—Early and accurate detection of plant diseases is
crucial for ensuring food security and minimizing crop losses.
Deep learning-based object detection models, particularly those
based on the You Only Look Once (YOLO) architecture, have
shown promise in this area. This study compares the perfor-
mance of five recent YOLO models - YOLOv5, YOLOv5-p6,
YOLOv6, YOLOv8, and YOLOv9 - for plant disease detection
using the PlantDoc dataset. The models were evaluated based
on their accuracy, speed, and resource requirements. Our
results indicate that YOLOv9-C and YOLOv8s demonstrated
superior performance in terms of F1-Score, Recall, and mAP
metrics. These models achieved an F1-Score of 0.39 and 0.37
respectively, with YOLOv9-C leading in Recall at 0.51 and
both models tying at a Best mAP@50 of 46%. Moreover, the
highest F1-Score was achieved by YOLOv5s at 0.42, indicating
that this model also has potential for further exploration and
optimization. The highest mAP50-95 was achieved by YOLOv8s
at 36%, suggesting that this model may be particularly effective
at detecting a wide range of plant diseases across different
thresholds.

I. INTRODUCTION
Plant diseases pose a significant threat to global food
security [1], causing substantial crop losses and economic
damage. Early and accurate detection of diseases is crucial
for implementing timely and effective management strategies
to mitigate these losses and ensure food sustainability [2].
Traditional methods for disease detection rely on visual
inspection by experts, which is time-consuming, subjective,
and often impractical for large-scale applications. The de-
velopment of deep learning models has revolutionized plant
disease detection by offering automated, accurate, and effi-
cient solutions [3]. These models can analyze large datasets
of images with high accuracy and speed, surpassing the
limitations of manual inspection [4]. In particular, object de-
tection algorithms have proven highly effective in identifying
and localizing disease symptoms on plants. Among object
detection algorithms, one-stage detectors like the You Only
Look Once (YOLO) [5] series offer significant advantages
over two-stage detectors. One-stage detectors perform object
detection and classification in a single step, resulting in
faster inference speeds crucial for real-time applications.
This is in contrast to two-stage detectors, which first gen-
erate region proposals and then classify them, leading to
slower processing times. For example, YOLOv5 can achieve
real-time detection speeds of over 100 frames per second
(FPS), while a two-stage detector like Faster R-CNN [6]
typically operates at around 10 FPS. This speed advantage
makes YOLO models particularly suitable for deployment on
resource-constrained devices in the field. This study presents
a comparative analysis of five recent YOLO models for
plant disease detection: YOLOv5, YOLOv5-P6, YOLOv6,
YOLOv8, and YOLOv9. These models were evaluated on
the PlantDoc dataset, which contains images of various
plant species with diverse disease and healthy conditions.
This study aims to provide valuable insights for researchers
and practitioners in selecting the optimal YOLO model for
plant disease detection based on their specific requirements
and constraints. By analyzing the performance of these
models across different metrics, this study can guide the
development and deployment of efficient and accurate plant
disease detection systems, contributing to improved crop
health and global food security.
II. YOLO (YOU ONLY LOOK ONCE) AND FRAMEWORKS
YOLO (You Only Look Once) represents a seminal frame-
work in the realm of real-time object detection. Unlike its

predecessors, YOLO reframes object detection as a regres-
sion problem, directly predicting bounding boxes and class
probabilities from full images within a single evaluation
[7]. This unified approach circumvents the complexities of
traditional multi-stage object detectors. While YOLO can
be implemented within various frameworks, Darknet was the
most common framework for versions prior to YOLOv5 [8].
Successive iterations of YOLO (from YOLOv5 to YOLOv9)
have demonstrated continuous evolution and refinement.
These enhancements often focus on architectural modifica-
tions, novel loss functions, and advanced training techniques.
YOLO’s speed and adaptability have propelled its adoption
across diverse applications, including autonomous vehicles,
medical image analysis, and precision agriculture [9] [10].

A. YOLOv5
YOLOv5 [11] built upon the strengths of its predecessors
while offering greater flexibility and ease of use. It pro-
vided various model sizes (Nano, Small, Medium, Large,
XLarge) to cater to different speed-accuracy requirements
and adopted the PyTorch framework for easier implemen-
tation and modification. YOLOv5 also implemented Effi-
cientDet [12] inspired scaling rules for adjusting model
size and employed the YOLOv3 head for objectness and
classification prediction. Due to its ease of use, flexibility,
and performance, YOLOv5 became widely adopted within
the computer vision community.
B. YOLOv6
YOLOv6 [13] focused on deployment efficiency while
maintaining competitive performance. It introduced the
RepVGG backbone [14], which utilizes re-parameterization
for efficient inference, and replaced the YOLOv3 head
with a decoupled head for objectness and classification.
Additionally, YOLOv6 utilized SimOTA label assignment
for improved target selection and employed various training
techniques like head decoupling and auxiliary head to boost
accuracy.
C. YOLOv8
YOLOv8 [15] introduced a unified architecture for ob-
ject detection and instance segmentation, employing task-
specific heads for different tasks while sharing a common
backbone and neck. It utilized an anchor-free detection head
for efficient inference and offered various model sizes for
different speed-accuracy trade-offs. YOLOv8 achieved com-
petitive performance in both object detection and instance
segmentation tasks.
D. YOLOv9
YOLOv9 [16] focused on addressing information loss dur-
ing the feedforward process in deep networks. It introduced
the concept of programmable gradient information (PGI) to
generate reliable gradients and designed the Generalized Ef-
ficient Layer Aggregation Network (GELAN) for improved
parameter utilization and accuracy. Notably, YOLOv9 uti-
lized conventional convolution operators to achieve better
performance than depth-wise convolution based designs. It
achieved state-of-the-art performance on the MS COCO
dataset, surpassing previous train-from-scratch methods.

manual labeling of five previously unlabeled images, and
the provision of format conversion capabilities for seamless
integration with various YOLO versions and other machine
learning frameworks. These modifications augmented the
original PlantDoc dataset—comprising 2669 images across

B. Training, Validation and performance metrics
The YOLO models (v5, v5-p6, v6, v7, v8 and v9) were
subjected to a training process utilizing the dataset described
in this study. This dataset was partitioned into a training set
and a validation set as described in table II to ensure robust
model training and evaluation. The training process for each
model was executed employing the pre-trained weights given
by the developers of YOLO. The YOLO configuration file
facilitates the alteration of hyperparameters. However, in
the context of this study, we opted to employ the default
hyperparameters across all models.

plant diseases.

The Best Confidence Threshold in the context of object
detection models refers to the optimal point on the Precision-
Recall curve that yields the highest F1 score. The F1 score is
the harmonic mean of precision and recall, and it measures
the balance between these two metrics. The calculation
of the area beneath the curve was executed utilizing the
mAP@50 metric. As illustrated in Figure 4B, the confidence
thresholds underwent optimization and adjustment to ensure
the F-1 score for each model was at its peak. This process
underscores the importance of fine-tuning model parameters
to achieve optimal performance.Following this optimization
process, the optimal confidence thresholds were determined
to be 0.20, 0.11, 0.07, 0.31, and 0.18 for the v5, v5-p6, v6,
v8 and v9 models, respectively 5.
Precision-recall curves [19] provide a visual representa-
tion of the trade-off between precision and recall at different
decision thresholds. A model with high precision but low
recall may be conservative in its predictions, while a model
with high recall but low precision may be more aggressive
in identifying diseased plants. Figure 6 shows the preci-
sion–recall curves for each trained YOLO model.
V. DISCUSSION
The benchmark results for plant disease recognition using
various YOLO models on the PlantDoc dataset provide
insightful observations. Among the evaluated models, the
YOLOv9-C and YOLOv8s models have demonstrated su-
perior performance in terms of F1-Score, Recall, and mAP
metrics. Specifically, YOLOv9-C achieved an F1-Score of
0.39 and a leading Recall of 0.51, while both YOLOv9-C
and YOLOv8s tied for the highest Best mAP@50 of 46%.

These metrics indicate a promising direction for enhancing
the accuracy and efficiency of plant disease identification
using these advanced YOLO models.
Interestingly, the YOLOv5s model attained the highest
overall F1-Score at 0.42, highlighting its potential for fur-
ther exploration and optimization. Additionally, the highest
mAP50-95 was recorded by YOLOv8s at 36%, suggest-
ing that this model is particularly effective at detecting a
wide range of plant diseases across different thresholds.
This versatility across varying mAP thresholds underscores
YOLOv8s’s robustness in diverse detection scenarios.
These results collectively suggest that while YOLOv9-
C and YOLOv8s excel in certain metrics, YOLOv5s also
shows considerable promise, particularly in achieving a
higher F1-Score. This variability across different models
indicates that the choice of YOLO architecture may depend
on specific application needs and performance priorities.
VI. FUTURE DIRECTIONS
Future research could focus on several key areas to build
upon these findings:
• Model Optimization:
– Architecture Exploration: Further investigation into
alternative architectures and modifications to the
YOLO framework could yield improvements in
detection performance. This could include experi-
menting with different layers, activation functions,
and hyperparameter tuning.
– Feature Integration: Incorporating additional fea-
tures such as multi-spectral imaging data or en-
vironmental factors (e.g., humidity, temperature)
could enhance model accuracy. The integration of
domain-specific knowledge about plant diseases
might also contribute to better performance.
• Dataset Expansion:
– Diverse Plant Species: Expanding the dataset to
include a broader range of plant species and dis-
ease types can improve the model’s generalization
capabilities. Ensuring a balanced representation of
different disease severities and stages is crucial.
– High-Resolution Imagery: Utilizing higher resolu-
tion images might help in capturing finer details,
aiding in the detection of early-stage diseases
which are often more challenging to identify.
• Real-Time Application:
– Field Deployment: Developing lightweight, real-
time versions of these models suitable for deploy-
ment on mobile devices or drones can facilitate
early detection in agricultural settings. This would
enable farmers to take timely action, thereby min-
imizing crop loss and enhancing food security.
– Automated Monitoring Systems: Integrating these
models into automated monitoring systems can
provide continuous surveillance of crops, leading
to more proactive and precise disease management.
• Transfer Learning and Domain Adaptation:
– Transfer Learning: Applying transfer learning
techniques to leverage pre-trained models on
larger, more diverse datasets can enhance the detec-
tion performance on specific plant disease datasets
like PlantDoc.

– Domain Adaptation: Adapting models trained on
synthetic or related datasets to the target domain
(real-world agricultural settings) can improve their
robustness and applicability.
• Collaborative Approaches:
– Interdisciplinary
Collaboration:
Collaborating
with plant pathologists, agronomists, and data
scientists can provide a multidisciplinary approach
to refining these models. This collaboration can
ensure that the models are both scientifically
accurate and practically useful.
VII. CONCLUSION
These benchmark results provide a valuable foundation
for future research in plant disease recognition using YOLO
models. By continuing to refine and optimize these models,
we can strive towards more accurate, efficient, and real-
time plant disease detection, contributing to sustainable
agriculture and food security. The findings provide valuable
guidance for selecting the optimal YOLO model for plant
disease detection based on specific needs and constraints.
Future research can explore further optimization and cus-
tomization of these models for specific plant species and
diseases. This endeavor aligns with the broader objective
of harnessing the power of artificial intelligence to address
pressing global challenges.
